# Chapter 7: dx — Leibniz and the Operational Infinite

Leibniz never gets the full reward he is due. Newton gave calculus geometric force. Berkeley gave it philosophical trouble. Leibniz did something that now looks especially modern: he made change operational. He built a symbolic machinery through which the continuous could be handled, transformed, and worked upon. That is why `dx` still matters. It is not only a mark in the history of mathematics. It is part of the long prehistory of the transformer.

Newton argued in geometric terms. His achievement was immense: motion, flux, and physical law were given mathematical form. Berkeley argued in philosophical terms. He pressed on the ontological weakness of infinitesimals, exposing the tension between operational success and conceptual clarity. Leibniz argued in operational terms. He asked how change could be written, manipulated, and reasoned with through symbols. His notational genius did not eliminate the difficulty of the continuous, but it made that difficulty tractable. He gave thought a disciplined way to work on what could not be held all at once.

This did not happen on a Wednesday in 1687. The power of calculus arrived before its deepest settlement. It took years of argument, then generations of refinement, before later mathematics clarified what the early calculus had made usable. Rigor was sharpened. Limits were formalized. The structure of the continuous became more intelligible. Riemann, among others, helped capture a deeper geometric essence that earlier thinkers had approached through notation, intuition, and dispute. What began as symbolic power gradually became conceptual architecture.

That history matters because philosophy never left the building. We live in the continuous—time, motion, change, physical process—but we compute in the discrete. Whenever we model reality, we do so through representation, approximation, and limits. Calculus made that bridge operational, but it did not dissolve the tension underneath it. Berkeley is still here because the old question remains: what exactly is the relation between the world as it unfolds and the forms through which we compute it?

This is why Leibniz belongs in the history of AI. Modern machine learning depends on the operationalization of change. Gradients, optimization, and continuous-valued representations all inherit a world in which local transformation can be symbolized, computed, and used. Transformers may be described in the language of vectors, manifolds, and differential geometry, but they remain part of this much older lineage. Their paper may have appeared in 2017. Their history began long before that.

The transformer may have been born on a Wednesday in 2017, but its history began a very long time ago. Long before attention maps and loss functions, there was `dx`: a sign that made change operable. That is why Leibniz matters here. He did not merely contribute to mathematics. He helped create the civilizational habit of turning process into symbolic machinery. And once that habit exists, an age of intelligent machines becomes thinkable.
