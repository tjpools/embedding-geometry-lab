## Reader Entry

Chapter 2 constructed vectors as numerical representations. Chapter 3 showed one distribution changing through likelihood weighting and normalization. The reader may still treat every numerical transformation as linear, a matrix as the transformation itself rather than a coordinate representation, or a derivative as a global description.

## Intended Exit

The reader can distinguish:

- a linear map from its matrix in selected coordinates
- a vector from its transformed output
- linearity from arbitrary numerical transformation
- a scalar derivative, gradient, and Jacobian
- global transformation from local first-order approximation
- analytic differentiation from finite-difference checking
- derivative information from an optimization or learning rule

## Central Question

How can a system transform numerical representations and describe how a nonlinear transformation changes near one point?

## Chapter Claim

A matrix can represent a linear map that preserves addition and scalar multiplication. For a differentiable nonlinear map, the Jacobian at a declared point is a local linear map from small input changes to first-order output changes. Its scope is local and conditional on the specified function, coordinates, and point.

## Chapter Result

The fixed matrix passes sampled additivity and homogeneity checks exactly. For

$$
f(x,y)=\left(x+\frac14y^2,\sin x+y\right),
$$

at $p=(0.6,-0.8)$ in direction $d=(0.3,-0.2)$, the analytic prediction is

$$
J_f(p)d=(0.38,0.0476006845).
$$

Central finite-difference error decreases from approximately $3.7\times10^{-5}$ at $h=10^{-1}$ to $3.7\times10^{-11}$ at $h=10^{-4}$.

## Visual Anchor

**A Map and Its Local Change** places two related structures on one field: a regular coordinate grid transformed by the fixed matrix, and a small neighborhood around $p$ transformed by the nonlinear map. A tangent-grid overlay generated by $J_f(p)$ shows local agreement without implying global identity.

**Structural reveal:** a matrix can describe a complete linear transformation, while a Jacobian describes first-order change only near its evaluation point.

The visual must identify the function, point, direction, matrix, and local scope without relying on color.
