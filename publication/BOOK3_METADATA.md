## Short Description

What does it mean to derive rather than retrieve meaning? Beginning with Berkeley's questions about perception and signs, this book examines how geometry, constraints, transformers, tools, and human judgment shape what inquiry can reach and what interpretation may responsibly claim.

## KDP Description

What happens when a human question enters into structured contact with a transformer?

*The Architecture of Geometric Semantics* begins with George Berkeley's questions about perception, signs, ideas, and what warrants talk of an object. It brings that historical pressure into contact with modern transformer architecture, learned representation, geometry, programming systems, provenance, and human judgment.

The book does not claim that Berkeley anticipated artificial intelligence, that meaning is literally spatial, or that a model authors its own interpretations. It asks a more disciplined question: how is meaning derived through constrained interaction among human intention, inherited language, machine transformation, tools, and verification?

Moving from closure and trajectories to interpretation and responsibility, Terrence J McLaughlin develops a philosophy of inquiry in which architecture supplies the evidence, assumptions remain visible, and fluent output never substitutes for provenance.

Meaning is not merely retrieved. It is derived through structured contact with lineage.

## Back-of-Book Copy

Meaning is often described as though it were stored intact: inside a word, a mind, a text, or a machine. *The Architecture of Geometric Semantics* begins elsewhere.

George Berkeley's questions about perception, signs, ideas, and objects provide the historical pressure. Transformer architecture provides a modern site of inspection. Between them lies a deeper inquiry into closure, geometry, learned relation, interpretation, provenance, and responsibility.

This book asks what becomes possible when a person structures a question, brings it into contact with learned patterns, tests the continuation against evidence, and returns with a better question. It distinguishes collaboration from authorship, learned association from citation, geometric models from ontology, and persuasive language from established truth.

Berkeley is the protagonist of the question, not a voice inside the machine. The transformer extends relational reach, but interpretation and accountability remain human.

Architecture supplies the evidence. Philosophy states the assumptions. Interpretation remains accountable.
