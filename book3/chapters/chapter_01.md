# Chapter 1 — Berkeley's Question

A method reaches the right answer. What, exactly, has succeeded?

The answer may seem obvious. A correct result appears to vindicate the operation that produced it. Repetition strengthens the impression: if the method continues to calculate, predict, or guide action reliably, perhaps its objects are clear, its reasoning is sound, and its interpretation is secure.

George Berkeley begins from the possibility that these achievements can come apart.

In 1734 he addressed *The Analyst* to an “Infidel Mathematician.” The title matters. Berkeley was not conducting a detached modern audit of mathematical technique. He was intervening in a religious controversy, challenging mathematicians who demanded rigor in theology while, in his view, tolerating obscurity in their own foundations. The polemical target shaped the work's rhetoric. It does not, however, erase the distinction on which the argument turns.

Berkeley granted that the new fluxional methods produced remarkable results. His dispute concerned what those results licensed anyone to say about the reasoning behind them:

> I have no Controversy about your Conclusions, but only about your Logic and Method. [...] It must be remembered that I am not concerned about the truth of your Theorems, but only about the way of coming at them; whether it be legitimate or illegitimate, clear or obscure, scientific or tentative.

The issue in *The Analyst* §20 is not whether a theorem happens to be true. It is whether the path to it supplies the clarity and evidence claimed for a scientific demonstration. Berkeley sharpens the challenge in Queries 35 and 36:

> Whether there be not a way of arriving at Truth, although the Principles are not scientific, nor the Reasoning just? And whether such a way ought to be called a Knack or a Science?
>
> Whether there can be Science of the Conclusion, where there is not Evidence of the Principles?

These are questions in a polemic, not a settled theory of knowledge. They do not show that calculus was useless, that every fluxional derivation was defective, or that correct conclusions generally arise by accident. Later mathematics would formulate rigorous approaches unavailable to Berkeley's immediate targets. Nor does the historical record support a triumphal story in which Berkeley alone forced that development. The narrower pressure survives: a successful conclusion and a warranted derivation are not identical achievements.

That pressure opens this book. It does not yet answer it.

## The Object Behind the Operation

Berkeley's concern about mathematical reasoning belongs to a larger inquiry into what words, signs, and ideas allow us to claim. More than two decades before *The Analyst*, the Introduction to *A Treatise Concerning the Principles of Human Knowledge* attacked the doctrine of abstract general ideas.

The target was not general thought itself. Berkeley did not deny that a particular triangle could stand for triangles generally, or that a word could be used across many instances. His objection concerned the supposed mental object made by stripping away every determinate feature: a triangle that is neither equilateral, isosceles, nor scalene, and yet somehow all and none of these at once. In *Principles*, Introduction 6–16, he asks whether such an idea can actually be formed rather than merely named.

This distinction is easy to flatten. One could turn Berkeley into a theorist for whom every meaningful word must call up one determinate image. His own discussion resists that reading. In Introduction 16 and 19–20, he allows particular signs to function generally and allows language to affect conduct without continually presenting a corresponding idea to the mind. The fact that a sign can be used does not prove that every object supposedly named by it has been conceived. But neither does the absence of one fixed image make the sign meaningless.

The result is a double constraint. We should not invent an object merely because a grammatical form invites us to do so. We should also not assume that meaningful use always consists in displaying a private picture attached to each word. Berkeley's anti-abstractionism creates pressure on object-talk without reducing language to image retrieval.

This is why the calculus case cannot be isolated from the rest of his philosophy. A technique can manipulate signs successfully while a dispute remains about the objects those signs purport to represent. Conversely, a failure to produce one imaginable object does not by itself invalidate every disciplined use of the sign. Operation, representation, and ontology must be examined separately before they can be reconnected.

## A World of Ideas and Spirits

Berkeley's own reconnection is metaphysical and theological. Any attempt to preserve his questions while hiding that answer would misrepresent the historical position.

In *Principles* 1–4, Berkeley begins with the objects of human knowledge: ideas given in sense, ideas perceived through reflection on the mind's operations and passions, and ideas formed through memory and imagination. Sensible things are collections of ideas. A cherry is not an unknowable material support concealed behind color, taste, shape, and texture. It is the ordered sensible object composed through them.

This does not mean that reality collapses into private fantasy. Berkeley distinguishes ideas of sense from ideas of imagination by their force, order, and independence from an individual's will. In *Principles* 29–33, their stability belongs to the laws of nature. The perceiving and acting beings in this account are spirits, not ideas. God grounds the regular order through which sensible ideas are given and coordinated.

The *Three Dialogues* develops this position as an answer to skepticism and as a defense of ordinary sensible bodies. Philonous argues that error need not lie in what is immediately perceived. It can enter through a judgment about what other ideas will accompany or follow the present appearance. That point is paraphrased here from the Third Dialogue rather than quoted: the standard Luce–Jessop page has not yet been directly inspected. The evidential limitation changes how the passage may enter this manuscript, not whether the limitation is visible.

Berkeley's master argument presses a stronger claim. In *Principles* 22–23 and the Dialogues, he argues that one cannot conceive a sensible object existing unconceived, because the attempted conception is itself an act of conceiving. A familiar objection replies that this confuses a representation with the represented situation: imagining an unperceived tree need not include an imagined observer within the scene. The objection marks a live dispute, not a detail to be removed for narrative momentum. Berkeley's critique can expose a problem in the opponent's account without thereby establishing every part of immaterialism.

His positive commitments therefore matter twice. Historically, they prevent the chapter from secularizing Berkeley into a convenient precursor. Philosophically, they show that criticism of one proposed object does not end inquiry. Berkeley does not stop at saying that material substance is obscure. He offers ideas, spirits, divine governance, and laws of nature as an alternative account. Whether that account succeeds remains contestable.

## Rules That Reach Beyond the Present

Berkeley's laws of nature preserve a substantial role for inquiry. Natural philosophy does not disclose material efficient causes behind appearances, but it can discover regularities, connect phenomena, and extend expectation. In *Principles* §105 he writes that

> [R]ules grounded on the Analogy, and Uniformness observed in the Production of natural Effects [...] extend our Prospect beyond what is present, and near to us, and enable us to make very probable Conjectures [...] as well as to predict Things to come.

Prediction is not dismissed as a lesser or empty achievement. Observed uniformity permits investigators to move beyond the present case. General rules organize phenomena and support probable conjecture. Within Berkeley's system, that order expresses divine governance rather than a mind-independent material mechanism. Even so, the operational achievement is genuine: natural rules can guide successful expectation.

The permission is paired with a warning. In §§106 and 108 Berkeley says:

> But we should proceed warily in such Things: for we are apt to lay too great a Stress on Analogies, and to the prejudice of Truth, humour that Eagerness of the Mind, whereby it is carried to extend its Knowledge into general Theorems.
>
> [I]n arguing from general Rules of Nature, it is not impossible we may extend the Analogy too far, and by that means run into Mistakes.

Analogy enables inquiry because similarities and regularities let one case inform another. Analogy also creates risk because the mind can carry a relation beyond the evidence that first supported it. Berkeley is not rejecting generalization. He is locating one way that disciplined generalization can become overreach.

That distinction is especially important here. A historical analogy can identify a question that recurs under changed conditions. It cannot transfer a historical thinker's authority to a modern answer. Similarity supplies a route for examination, not proof that the compared objects share an ontology, mechanism, or normative status.

## The Language of Vision

Berkeley's *Essay Towards a New Theory of Vision* gives the predictive role of signs an embodied form. Distance is not immediately seen, he argues in §§2–17. Visual cues suggest tangible ideas through connections learned in experience. The relation is not necessary in the way a geometric entailment is necessary. It becomes reliable through an ordered course of perception.

The visual sign matters because of what it enables a perceiver to anticipate and do. In §147 Berkeley writes:

> [T]he proper Objects of Vision constitute an universal Language of the Author of Nature, whereby we are instructed how to regulate our Actions, in order to attain those things, that are necessary to the Preservation and Well-being of our Bodies, as also to avoid whatever may be hurtful and destructive of them.

This is not a secular theory of signs in computational systems. The language is the language of the Author of Nature. Its interpreters are embodied perceivers whose conduct concerns preservation, well-being, and harm. Sections 45, 59, and 85–87 supply the learned connections and practical foresight behind the statement.

The passage nevertheless establishes something precise within Berkeley's account. A sign can guide action through a learned, regular relation without resembling what it signifies and without making the signified object immediately present. The action-guiding success is real. Its warrant depends on the stability of the relation, the context in which it was learned, and the ends toward which the action is directed.

The boundary is as important as the correspondence. Visual ideas, words, algebraic marks, and physical terms are not automatically the same kind of sign. Berkeley's treatment of one relation cannot be exported unchanged to every representational system. Even within vision, a reliable cue does not guarantee an infallible judgment in every circumstance. Order supports expectation; it does not eliminate interpretation.

## Calculation Is Not Ontology

In *De Motu*, Berkeley brings the distinction between operational service and ontological description into mechanics. Discussing force and its mathematical resolution, he argues in §18:

> These things serve mechanics and computation: but it is one thing to serve computation and mathematical demonstrations, and another to exhibit the nature of things.

The sentence is direct, but its scope is not unlimited. Berkeley is discussing mathematical treatments of force, not declaring that every successful scientific term is a fiction. In §§35–36 he accepts explanation through simple, general principles grounded in phenomena. Scholarship consequently permits more than one reconstruction. Some passages support a restricted instrumentalist reading of Berkeley on Newtonian dynamics. Reductionist readings instead emphasize his attempt to restate force-talk in terms of laws, motions, and observable relations. Neither warrants classifying his whole philosophy as instrumentalism.

The limited conclusion is stronger because it is limited. A term may perform indispensable work in calculation without that performance alone settling what kind of thing the term names. Conversely, refusal to infer an ontology directly from calculation does not deprive calculation of rigor or utility. Different questions demand different warrants.

Across these cases, Berkeley's documented position has its own shape. Fluxional conclusions can be true while their derivations remain disputed. Words can function generally without producing the abstract objects philosophers suppose. Sensible objects remain real within an ontology of ideas and spirits. Natural laws support prediction under divine governance. Learned visual signs guide embodied action. Mechanical terms can serve computation without automatically exhibiting the nature of things.

No single doctrine unifies these claims without controversy. Their differences should remain visible. The calculus dispute concerns logical method. Anti-abstractionism concerns what can be conceived and how signs function. Vision concerns learned expectation. Natural philosophy concerns law, prediction, and explanation. Immaterialism concerns what exists. Treating all of them as instances of one modern theory would repeat the overextension Berkeley warned against.

## Book Three Reconstruction

The following sentence belongs to Book Three rather than Berkeley:

> **Demonstrated operation constrains interpretation, but does not by itself settle ontology, meaning, or warranted action.**

This reconstruction draws a question from the historical cases while refusing to make Berkeley answer it in modern terms. The assumption connecting them is explicit: when an operation succeeds, its success is evidence for some claims about the operation, but the scope of that evidence depends on what was tested, represented, and inferred. A true conclusion may not certify every premise. A useful sign may not establish the object attributed to it. A reliable prediction may not disclose an underlying ontology. An action-guiding relation may not determine whether an action is responsible.

The strongest objection is that this reconstruction risks becoming trivial. Of course a calculation does not answer every philosophical or practical question. But the problem is not merely that different questions exist. Operational success often travels rhetorically. A working result lends credibility to a description of what the system represents, then to an interpretation of what that representation means, and finally to a recommendation about how people should rely on it. Each movement can be reasonable. None is licensed solely by the success that began the sequence.

A second objection points in the other direction. If interpretation always asks for more than operation can supply, perhaps the demand for warrant becomes impossible to satisfy. Berkeley does not support that conclusion. His natural rules permit probable conjecture and prediction. His visual signs guide embodied conduct. The question is not whether operation counts as evidence. It is what the operation is evidence of, what further premises connect it to a claim, and where judgment enters.

This is the first philosophical operation of Book Three: distinguish demonstrated performance from the ontology, meaning, and action attributed to it. The present evidence boundary is historical. Berkeley supplies cases and arguments showing why the distinction requires examination. He does not supply this book's closure contract, its account of derived meaning, or its allocation of responsibility.

At the operator level, Chapter 1 exports a provisional audit rather than a theory. Identify the **operation** that was performed, the **result** observed under stated conditions, the **interpretation** added to that result, and the **claim** the combined record is being asked to support. Then ask which step established each part. This gloss does not yet define closure across algebraic, programmed, learned, institutional, or philosophical domains; Chapters 4 and 8 own those distinctions. Its purpose here is narrower: prevent success at one step from silently supplying warrant at another.

The chapter's closure status is therefore **withheld**. Its bounded evidence consists of the verified historical passages, their scholarly disputes, and the limited operational findings inherited from Book Two. The unresolved judgment is what additional warrant permits movement from successful operation to ontology, meaning, or action. Chapter 1 introduces no values, assigns no decision authority, and authorizes no action. It passes the question forward rather than disguising an unfinished inquiry as closure.

## Contemporary Application

Only now can the transformer enter the inquiry.

Book Two built, tested, and reverse engineered a bounded machine. Those tests established operational facts under declared conditions. They did not become weaker because they stopped at their evidential boundary. Nor may Book Three strengthen them into claims the tests did not perform.

Berkeley's cases provide historical pressure, not a verdict on that machine. A transformer is not a fluxional calculus, a visual language, or a Berkeleyan system of ideas. Its learned numerical representations are not eighteenth-century abstract ideas. The analogy is qualified and narrow: in both inquiries, demonstrated use can tempt an interpreter to move too quickly from what works to what its objects are, what its signs mean, or what action their outputs warrant.

The closure contract will later represent the human transition from bounded evidence to action as

$$
(E,V,J,R)\rightarrow A.
$$

Chapter 1 does not execute that transition. It identifies the unresolved question that makes the later record necessary. Evidence constrains what may responsibly be claimed, but evidence does not silently choose values, exercise judgment, assign authority, accept consequences, or define revision. Those additions must become visible when the book reaches them.

Berkeley therefore opens Book Three neither as its hidden architect nor as a source of borrowed authority. He opens it as a difficult historical interlocutor. His arguments force a distinction between arriving and knowing how one arrived, between using a sign and establishing its object, between predicting an effect and exhibiting the nature of things.

The question carried forward is exact: what warrants the movement from a successful operation to an interpretation of its objects, signs, and results?

Chapter 2 turns to Leibniz, for whom symbolic form and relational order promise a more ambitious answer. If Berkeley makes object-talk contestable, Leibniz asks how much intelligibility form itself can provide. The dream of form begins where Berkeley's question remains open.
