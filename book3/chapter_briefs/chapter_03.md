## Reader Entry

The reader arrives from Chapter 2 able to see formal relation as historical pressure without endorsement of a completed universal method. Berkeley has made object-talk contestable and Leibniz has raised the promise and limit of symbolic form. Neither settles what makes structured experience intelligible in the first place.

No prior knowledge of Kant, transcendental philosophy, the categories, or the phenomenon–noumenon distinction is assumed.

## Intended Exit

The reader can distinguish:

- Kant's account of sensibility, understanding, and synthesis from later theories of representation and computation
- the a priori forms of intuition and the categories of the understanding from data structures, features, or hardware
- the transcendental unity of apperception from an implementation of an integrated system
- phenomena and noumena from surface outputs and hidden internal states of a machine
- the limits Kant argues from what Book Three reconstructs as the question that conditions of possibility carry forward
- the A edition (1781) from the B edition (1787) of the *Critique of Pure Reason* wherever the two diverge on load-bearing claims

The reader does not need to accept Kant's transcendental idealism to recognize that inquiry has conditions that neither perception nor formal relation supplies on its own.

## Central Question

What must already be in place for structured experience and objective judgment to be possible at all, and how do those conditions constrain what any later account of derivation may claim?

## Chapter Claim

Kant treats objective knowledge as jointly conditioned by sensibility, understanding, and the synthetic unity that binds them. Space and time are the a priori forms of intuition, the categories organize what is intuited into objects of judgment, and the transcendental unity of apperception is the condition under which any manifold can be recognized as a manifold at all. These conditions are not psychological reports and not descriptions of a mechanism. They mark limits: the categories apply only to objects of possible experience, and reason overreaches when it attempts to determine noumena, cosmological totalities, or the soul as objects of theoretical knowledge.

This chapter establishes conditions and limits as a question that any later account of derivation must respect. It does not claim that Kant anticipated formal semantics, cognitive science, transformer computation, or Book Three's thesis of derived meaning.

## Historical Distinctions

The chapter must keep four levels visibly separate:

1. **Primary text:** what Kant states or argues in a named work, section, and edition (A, B, or both).
2. **Scholarly interpretation:** how commentators reconstruct arguments whose formulations shift between editions and whose reception involves major disputes.
3. **Book Three reconstruction:** the narrower question this manuscript carries from transcendental conditions into its later account of derivation.
4. **Contemporary application:** a later comparison to formal systems, cognitive theory, or transformers, introduced only after Kant's position is established.

The proposed Book Three reconstruction is:

> Structured experience has conditions that neither perception nor formal relation supplies on its own, and any account of derived meaning must respect those conditions without impersonating them.

This sentence is not a Kant quotation or a summary of the transcendental philosophy. It remains provisional until the source ledger and attribution audit establish that the historical cases support it.

## Evidence Plan

Primary-source research must cover five distinct surfaces rather than infer one system from one famous phrase:

- **sensibility and intuition:** Transcendental Aesthetic in the *Critique of Pure Reason*, especially the metaphysical and transcendental expositions of space and time
- **understanding and the categories:** Metaphysical Deduction and the two versions of the Transcendental Deduction, including the A and B differences
- **synthesis and the unity of apperception:** the Deduction and the Schematism, with the transcendental unity of apperception explicitly identified
- **limits of knowledge:** the phenomenon–noumenon distinction in the Analytic, the Antinomies in the Dialectic, and the boundary drawn against dogmatic metaphysics
- **later reception of the conditions:** the *Prolegomena to Any Future Metaphysics* as Kant's own restatement, plus a specialist history covering the German critical edition, the Guyer–Wood English edition, and the standing A/B disputes

The minimum scholarly set must cover Kant on sensibility, understanding, synthesis, transcendental idealism, and at least one substantive objection to a cognitive-science or computational reconstruction of the transcendental project.

Research must prefer the Akademie-Ausgabe (AA) and a leading English critical edition. Online transcriptions may locate passages but may not become manuscript quotation authorities without edition-level comparison. Every proposed quotation must record the German original where load-bearing, its A and B locations, and the named English translation and edition page.

- Does every statement attributed to Kant identify a primary work, section, and edition, with the A and B pages named where they differ?
- Are direct quotations checked against a critical or inspected scholarly edition?
- Does the chapter present sensibility, understanding, synthesis, and limits as jointly conditioning objective knowledge rather than as one mechanism?
- Does it distinguish the Transcendental Aesthetic, Analytic, and Dialectic and refuse to compress them into a single argument?
- Are the A and B versions of the Transcendental Deduction treated as distinct texts?
- Does the chapter avoid translating Kantian terms directly into modern computational vocabulary?
- Are scholarly disputes about transcendental idealism, the deduction's success, and the phenomenon–noumenon distinction marked as disputes?
- Can the reader identify the exact point where documented history becomes Book Three's reconstruction?
- Does the chapter withhold closure, values, authority, action, and revision rather than claiming that conditions of possibility resolve them?

## Explicit Exclusions

This chapter does not:

- claim Kant anticipated computers, programming languages, artificial intelligence, transformers, embeddings, or vector semantics
- identify the categories with a schema, type system, feature set, or attention pattern
- present space and time as hardware or architecture, or the a priori as pre-training
- identify the transcendental unity of apperception with an integrated implementation or a control loop
- treat noumena as hidden internal states, latent representations, or ground-truth features of a model
- present the Antinomies as training-time failure modes or optimization pathologies
- collapse the A and B versions of the Transcendental Deduction into one statement
- treat the *Critique of Pure Reason* as a psychology, cognitive science, or algorithm
- hide transcendental idealism behind the epistemological claims that appear to travel independently of it
- claim that Kant established Book Three's account of closure, derived meaning, or warranted action
- use "conditions of possibility" as a rhetorical substitute for a completed mechanism, provenance, or responsibility
- authorize action or resolve the later relation among evidence, values, judgment, responsibility, and revision

## Closure Disposition

Chapter 3 does not authorize action. Its bounded evidence will consist of verified primary passages, edition records (A and B where relevant), and contested scholarly interpretations. Its unresolved question is what conditions of possibility permit or constrain any later account of derivation. It introduces no decision values, assigns no authority, and performs no transition to action. Closure remains withheld.
