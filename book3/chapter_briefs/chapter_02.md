## Reader Entry

The reader arrives from Chapter 1 able to distinguish a successful operation from the stronger claims made about its objects, signs, and results. Berkeley has made warranted object-talk contestable without supplying Book Three's answer.

No prior knowledge of Leibniz, early modern logic, possible-world semantics, or the history of calculus is assumed.

## Intended Exit

The reader can distinguish:

- Leibniz's documented projects for symbolic reasoning from later formal logic and computation
- the role of concepts, relations, expression, and systematic order in his account of intelligibility
- mathematical calculation from the broader project of a universal characteristic or calculus of reasoning
- complete concepts and possible worlds from modern computational state spaces
- what Leibniz argues from what Book Three reconstructs as the dream that form might make interpretation complete

The reader does not need to accept Leibniz's metaphysics or principle of sufficient reason to recognize the promise and pressure that formal relation introduces.

## Central Question

How much meaning can formal relation and symbolic order establish without an interpreter supplying further concepts, purposes, or judgment?

## Chapter Claim

Leibniz repeatedly treats intelligibility as structured: concepts contain relations, truths admit reasons, expressions preserve correspondences, and symbolic procedures can extend reasoning. These projects supply a historically specific pressure for Book Three: if relations can be made explicit and operations can be disciplined by form, how far can formal structure carry meaning before interpretation re-enters?

This chapter establishes the promise and limit as a question. It does not claim that Leibniz anticipated formal logic, digital computers, transformers, embeddings, possible-world semantics in its modern form, or Book Three's thesis of derived meaning.

## Historical Distinctions

The chapter must keep four levels visibly separate:

1. **Primary text:** what Leibniz states or argues in a named work, letter, manuscript, or stable section.
2. **Scholarly interpretation:** how editors and historians reconstruct projects whose texts, terminology, and chronology vary.
3. **Book Three reconstruction:** the narrower question this manuscript carries from Leibnizian form into its later account of derivation.
4. **Contemporary application:** a later comparison to formal systems, computation, or transformers, introduced only after Leibniz's position is established.

The proposed Book Three reconstruction is:

> Formal relations can constrain and extend interpretation without making interpretation mechanically complete.

This sentence is not a Leibniz quotation or a summary of his complete system. It remains provisional until the source ledger and attribution audit establish that the historical cases support it.

## Evidence Plan

Primary-source research must cover five distinct surfaces rather than infer one system from one famous phrase:

- **concept and truth:** *Meditations on Knowledge, Truth, and Ideas*; *Discourse on Metaphysics*, especially the complete-concept argument
- **expression and relation:** *What Is an Idea?* and related texts on expression, representation, and correspondence
- **analysis and symbolic method:** *General Inquiries about the Analysis of Concepts and Truths* and texts on analysis, synthesis, characteristic, or a calculus of reasoning
- **possible worlds and metaphysical order:** *Discourse on Metaphysics*, *Monadology*, and related correspondence or essays, with chronology and genre preserved
- **mathematical calculus:** an inspected mathematical text and a history-of-mathematics source that distinguish Leibniz's calculus from his logical and philosophical projects

The minimum scholarly set must cover Leibniz on concepts and truth, expression, symbolic or logical projects, possible worlds, philosophy of mathematics, and at least one substantive objection to a strong formalist or computational reconstruction.

Research must prefer critical or scholarly editions. Online transcriptions may locate passages but may not become manuscript quotation authorities without edition-level comparison. French, Latin, and German passages require the original language, translator attribution, and edition page before direct quotation.

## Explicit Exclusions

This chapter does not:

- claim Leibniz anticipated computers, programming languages, artificial intelligence, transformers, embeddings, or vector semantics
- treat *calculemus* as proof that all reasoning can be automated or every dispute mechanically decided
- identify the universal characteristic, calculus of reasoning, and differential calculus as one completed system
- present possible worlds as literal computational state spaces or databases
- infer that a complete concept eliminates interpretation, contingency, agency, or scholarly dispute
- claim that expression is identity, resemblance, retrieval, or a universal theory of representation
- treat the principle of sufficient reason as an algorithm that mechanically produces every explanation
- hide Leibniz's substances, perceptions, pre-established harmony, God, or divine selection of a possible world
- claim that symbolic form eliminates interpretation or makes meaning mechanically complete
- attribute Book Three's closure contract, derived-meaning thesis, or operator vocabulary to Leibniz
- use the historical sequence Leibniz → solvability, Newton → operation, Berkeley → closure as a documented correspondence
- authorize action or resolve the later relation among evidence, values, judgment, responsibility, and revision

## Closure Disposition

Chapter 2 does not authorize action. Its bounded evidence will consist of verified primary passages, edition records, and contested scholarly interpretations. Its unresolved question is how far formal relation warrants an interpretation. It introduces no decision values, assigns no authority, and performs no transition to action. Closure remains withheld.
