## Source Standard

This ledger separates Kant's texts, editorial reconstruction, scholarly interpretation, Book Three's reconstruction, and contemporary application. The separation is especially important because the *Critique of Pure Reason* exists in two editions (A, 1781; B, 1787) whose Transcendental Deduction, Paralogisms, and Refutation of Idealism differ on load-bearing points, and because Kant's transcendental idealism admits at least two live scholarly reconstructions ("two-objects" and "two-aspects").

Primary locations use the standard abbreviations below:

- **AA** — *Kants gesammelte Schriften* (Akademie-Ausgabe), edited by the Königlich Preußische / Deutsche Akademie der Wissenschaften, Berlin: Georg Reimer (later Walter De Gruyter), 1900–, cited by volume and page (e.g., 4:255)
- **A / B** — first (1781) and second (1787) editions of the *Critique of Pure Reason*, cited by original pagination as fixed by the Akademie edition (e.g., A19/B33)
- **CPR** — *Critique of Pure Reason*
- **Prolegomena** — *Prolegomena to Any Future Metaphysics That Will Be Able to Come Forward as a Science* (1783), AA IV
- **GW** — Paul Guyer and Allen W. Wood, trans. and eds., *Critique of Pure Reason*, in *The Cambridge Edition of the Works of Immanuel Kant* (Cambridge University Press, 1998)
- **KS** — Norman Kemp Smith, trans., *Critique of Pure Reason* (Macmillan, 1929; revised 1933)
- **Pluhar** — Werner S. Pluhar, trans., *Critique of Pure Reason* (Hackett, 1996)
- **Meiklejohn** — J. M. D. Meiklejohn, trans., *Critique of Pure Reason* (Henry G. Bohn, 1855; various reprints, public domain)

The Akademie-Ausgabe is the controlling German critical authority. A direct English quotation must identify its translator and be checked against Kant's German text at its A and B pages. Public-domain scans (Meiklejohn 1855; original 1781 A and 1787 B German editions on Internet Archive and korpora.org) may locate passages but do not by themselves establish scholarly-edition authority. No famous phrase, including *Gedanken ohne Inhalt sind leer, Anschauungen ohne Begriffe sind blind*, is admitted by reputation alone.

## Primary Sources

### S1 — Akademie-Ausgabe

Königlich Preußische (later Deutsche) Akademie der Wissenschaften, ed., *Kants gesammelte Schriften*, 29 vols., Berlin: Georg Reimer / Walter De Gruyter, 1900–.

- URL: https://korpora.zim.uni-duisburg-essen.de/kant/ (Bonner Kant-Korpus, hosting the AA volumes)
- Authority: Standard historical-critical German edition of Kant's writings; the reference edition for A and B pagination of the *Critique of Pure Reason* and for AA volume-and-page citation of every other work
- Supports: German text of the *Critique of Pure Reason* (1781, 1787), the *Prolegomena* (1783), and Kant's correspondence
- Limitation: The AA is a German critical authority. It does not establish an English translation or an uncontested interpretation, and its digital hosts vary in interface reliability and image quality.

### S2 — *Critique of Pure Reason*: Transcendental Aesthetic

Immanuel Kant, *Critique of Pure Reason*, Transcendental Aesthetic, A19–49 / B33–73.

- Stable locations: Metaphysical Exposition of the Concept of Space (A22–25 / B37–40); Transcendental Exposition of the Concept of Space (B40–41); Metaphysical Exposition of the Concept of Time (A30–32 / B46–48); Transcendental Exposition of the Concept of Time (B48–49); General Observations on Transcendental Aesthetic (A42–49 / B59–73)
- Authority: Primary text; German controlling authority in AA III (B) and AA IV (A)
- Supports: Space and time as a priori forms of sensibility; distinction between form and matter of appearance; transcendental ideality with empirical reality of space and time; boundary against treating space and time as properties of things in themselves
- Limitation: The Aesthetic is one part of the *Critique* and does not by itself establish the deduction of the categories, the schematism, or the limits of possible experience. Its arguments must be read alongside the Analytic and the Dialectic.

### S3 — *Critique of Pure Reason*: Transcendental Deduction (A and B)

Immanuel Kant, *Critique of Pure Reason*, Transcendental Analytic, A84–130 (A Deduction, 1781) and B116–169 (B Deduction, 1787).

- Stable locations: A Deduction at A84–130; B Deduction at B116–169; principle of apperception at B131–132; combination and unity at B130; "I think" that must be able to accompany all my representations at B131–132; formal versus material self-consciousness contrast at B133; house-perception case at B142 (subjective) and B162 (objective); understanding as legislator of nature at A125–126, B163, B165
- Authority: Primary text with two distinct editorial versions
- Supports: The categories as a priori concepts of the understanding; the argument that they are conditions of the possibility of experience; the transcendental unity of apperception; the metaphysical deduction linking categories to forms of judgment
- Limitation: The A and B versions differ in strategy and detail. Any load-bearing claim about the Deduction must identify which edition it draws on. Rohlf (S7) explicitly warns that the transcendental deduction is "one of the most complex and difficult texts in the history of philosophy" and admits many reconstructions.

### S4 — *Critique of Pure Reason*: Schematism and Analytic of Principles

Immanuel Kant, *Critique of Pure Reason*, Book II of the Analytic of Concepts and the Analytic of Principles, A130–235 / B169–294.

- Stable locations: Schematism of the Pure Concepts of the Understanding (A137–147 / B176–187); System of All Principles of the Pure Understanding (A148–235 / B187–294); Refutation of Idealism (B274–279, added in B)
- Authority: Primary text
- Supports: How categories connect to intuitions through transcendental schemata; the principles of the pure understanding (axioms of intuition, anticipations of perception, analogies of experience, postulates of empirical thought); Kant's response to skeptical idealism
- Limitation: The Refutation of Idealism was added only in the B edition and its interpretation is contested. The Analogies of Experience carry Kant's discussion of causation and substance, which cannot be transported directly into modern discussions of causal inference or state.

### S5 — *Critique of Pure Reason*: Phenomena/Noumena and the Dialectic

Immanuel Kant, *Critique of Pure Reason*, On the Ground of the Distinction of All Objects into Phenomena and Noumena (A235–260 / B294–315), and the Transcendental Dialectic, A293–704 / B349–732.

- Stable locations: phenomena/noumena distinction at A235–260 / B294–315; Antinomies of Pure Reason at A405–567 / B432–595; ideas of soul, world-whole, God as the traditional objects of rational psychology, cosmology, and theology
- Authority: Primary text
- Supports: The limitation of the categories to objects of possible experience; the transcendental illusion that generates the ideas of pure reason; the four Antinomies as inspectable cases of reason overreaching; the regulative rather than constitutive use of the ideas of reason
- Limitation: The interpretive disputes here are severe. The two-objects and two-aspects readings of transcendental idealism disagree on whether noumena are a distinct class of objects, a distinct aspect of the same objects, or an empty limiting concept. Rohlf (S7) records both readings without settling the dispute.

### S6 — *Prolegomena to Any Future Metaphysics*

Immanuel Kant, *Prolegomena zu einer jeden künftigen Metaphysik, die als Wissenschaft wird auftreten können* (1783), AA IV 253–383.

- Stable locations: AA IV 255–257 for the possibility of metaphysics; AA IV 260–265 for the analytic/synthetic distinction; AA IV 275–294 for pure natural science; AA IV 294–365 for the transcendental problems of metaphysics
- Authority: Kant's own restatement of the *Critique*'s program; German controlling authority in AA IV; English translation in the Cambridge Edition (Allison and Heath 2002)
- Supports: Kant's own summary of the transcendental project; distinct formulation of the problem "how are synthetic a priori judgments possible?"; the analytic method of the *Prolegomena* as complement to the synthetic method of the *Critique*
- Limitation: The *Prolegomena* was written to make the *Critique* accessible after reviewers misread it. It is Kant's own gloss, not an independent argument, and it does not resolve the A/B disputes about the Transcendental Deduction.

## Scholarly and Editorial Sources

### S7 — Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy: Immanuel Kant

Michael Rohlf, "Immanuel Kant," *The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*, first published May 20, 2010; substantive revision July 31, 2024.

- URL: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/kant/
- Accessed: August 14, 2026
- Inspected sections: 1 Life and works; 2 Kant's project in the Critique of Pure Reason (2.1 Crisis of the Enlightenment, 2.2 Copernican revolution); 3 Transcendental idealism (3.1 Two-objects, 3.2 Two-aspects); 4 The transcendental deduction (4.1 Self-consciousness, 4.2 Objectivity and judgment, 4.3 Law-giver of nature)
- Authority: Peer-reviewed specialist overview by a working Kant scholar
- Supports: The Copernican-revolution passage at Bxvi–xviii and the summary at Bxviii; the transcendental-idealism formulation at A42/B59–60; the principle of apperception at B131–132; the combination-and-unity claim at B130; the house-perception case at B142/B162; the understanding-as-legislator claim at A125–126 and B163, B165; the standing dispute between the two-objects reading (traceable to the 1782 Göttingen review by Garve and Feder) and the two-aspects reading in Allison 2004 and Langton 1998
- Specific findings: (a) Rohlf identifies the *Critique* as Kant's response to a specific "crisis of the Enlightenment," in which the sovereignty of reason risked undermining traditional moral and religious beliefs, so the transcendental project is not read as a proto-cognitive-science; (b) Rohlf explicitly warns that the transcendental deduction admits many different interpretations and provides only "one perspective on some of its main ideas"; (c) Rohlf treats transcendental idealism as the framework within which theoretical and practical philosophy fit together, not as an epistemology detachable from Kant's metaphysics of freedom
- Limitation: This is interpretation and synthesis, not primary evidence. Its formulations may not be attributed to Kant, and its rival two-objects and two-aspects readings must remain marked as live disputes.

### S8 — Cambridge Edition of the Works of Immanuel Kant

Paul Guyer and Allen W. Wood, general editors, *The Cambridge Edition of the Works of Immanuel Kant*, Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 1992–.

- Authority: Standard English scholarly edition, cited by AA volume and page in the margins of every volume; the *Critique of Pure Reason* volume (Guyer and Wood 1998) is the reference English translation for the AA
- Supports: English translations of the *Critique of Pure Reason* (Guyer and Wood 1998), the *Prolegomena* and *Theoretical Philosophy after 1781* (Allison and Heath 2002), and other works; each volume carries scholarly introductions and editorial notes
- Limitation: The Cambridge Edition is not lawfully page-inspectable via open web resources. Direct quotation requires a physical or licensed copy for page-level comparison against the AA. Where no Cambridge volume has been inspected, paraphrase may be supported by the AA and Rohlf's context but English quotation remains prohibited.

### S9 — Kemp Smith Commentary and Translation

Norman Kemp Smith, *A Commentary to Kant's Critique of Pure Reason*, second edition (New York: Humanities Press, 1923; 1992 reprint); Norman Kemp Smith, trans., *Critique of Pure Reason* (Macmillan, 1929; revised 1933).

- Authority: A standard twentieth-century Anglophone commentary and translation, still in use as a secondary reference
- Supports: A widely cited translation and commentary on the *Critique*; Rohlf (S7) lists both in the Kant entry's bibliography
- Limitation: The 1929/1933 translation is not open-access in most jurisdictions and has been substantially superseded by the Cambridge Edition for scholarly citation. Any use here must record the specific edition and page inspected.

### S10 — Public-Domain Access to the German Editions and Meiklejohn Translation

Immanuel Kant, *Critik der reinen Vernunft*, first edition, Riga: J. F. Hartknoch, 1781 (A); second, revised edition, Riga: J. F. Hartknoch, 1787 (B); J. M. D. Meiklejohn, trans., *Critique of Pure Reason*, London: Henry G. Bohn, 1855.

- URLs: Multiple public-domain scans on Internet Archive; Bonner Kant-Korpus at https://korpora.zim.uni-duisburg-essen.de/kant/ hosts the AA-collated German text
- Authority: Original-language public-domain German editions of the *Critique* and the earliest widely reproduced English translation
- Supports: Navigation to A and B pagination; independent verification of AA page images; a public-domain English rendering that may locate passages
- Limitation: The 1855 Meiklejohn translation predates the standard scholarly edition and is not admissible as English quotation authority for a manuscript that requires the Guyer–Wood standard. Public-domain German scans are original-text authorities but are not translations.

## Prohibited Inferences

The current sources do not warrant claims that:

- Kant anticipated computers, cognitive science, artificial intelligence, transformers, embeddings, or vector semantics
- the categories are a schema, a type system, a feature set, or an attention pattern
- space and time are hardware or architecture, or the a priori is a form of pre-training
- the transcendental unity of apperception is an implementation of an integrated system or control loop
- noumena are hidden internal states, latent representations, or ground-truth features of a model
- the Antinomies are training-time failure modes or optimization pathologies
- the A and B Deductions may be compressed into one statement without loss of meaning
- transcendental idealism can be reduced to an epistemology detachable from Kant's account of freedom and morality
- Kant endorsed Book Three's closure contract or theory of derived meaning
- "conditions of possibility" may be used as a rhetorical substitute for a completed mechanism, provenance, or responsibility
