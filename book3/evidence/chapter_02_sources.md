## Source Standard

This ledger separates Leibniz's texts, editorial reconstruction, scholarly interpretation, Book Three's reconstruction, and contemporary application. The separation is especially important because Leibniz left no single work that contains his system, revised positions across decades and audiences, and left many logical projects unpublished or incomplete.

Primary locations use the standard abbreviations below:

- **A** — *Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz: Sämtliche Schriften und Briefe*, cited by series, volume, and page
- **G** — C. I. Gerhardt, ed., *Die philosophischen Schriften von Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz*, cited by volume and page
- **GM** — C. I. Gerhardt, ed., *Leibnizens mathematische Schriften*, cited by volume and page
- **AG** — Roger Ariew and Daniel Garber, eds. and trans., *Philosophical Essays* (Hackett, 1989)
- **L** — Leroy E. Loemker, ed. and trans., *Philosophical Papers and Letters*, second edition (Reidel, 1969)
- **LLP** — G. H. R. Parkinson, ed. and trans., *Logical Papers* (Clarendon Press, 1966)
- **RB** — Peter Remnant and Jonathan Bennett, eds. and trans., *New Essays on Human Understanding* (Cambridge University Press, 1981)

The Akademie edition is the controlling original-language authority where the relevant text has appeared. For Q1–Q6, the inspected source is Series VI, volume 4 (Berlin, 1999), whose Latin or French text and German editorial apparatus establish wording, witnesses, editorial dates, variants, and bibliography. Q7 is controlled here by the 1684 *Acta Eruditorum* publication as reprinted in GM V. These sources do not establish an English translation or an uncontested interpretation. A direct English quotation must identify its translator and be checked against the original-language critical text. Web translations may locate passages and expose variant titles, but their wording may enter the manuscript only when their translator and source manuscript or edition are recorded. No famous phrase, including *calculemus*, is admitted by reputation alone.

## Primary Sources

### S1 — Akademie Edition

Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz, *Sämtliche Schriften und Briefe*, historical-critical edition jointly maintained by the Berlin-Brandenburg Academy of Sciences and Humanities and the Göttingen Academy of Sciences and Humanities.

- URL: https://www.leibnizedition.de/
- Relevant series: II, philosophical correspondence; VI, philosophical writings; VII, mathematical writings
- Authority: Continuing historical-critical edition of Leibniz's writings and correspondence
- Supports: Original-language text, chronology, manuscript identity, variants, and stable A citations for the chapter's primary evidence
- Limitation: Publication coverage remains incomplete, and the chapter must not infer a single settled system from texts separated by date, genre, audience, or editorial history.

### S2 — *Meditations on Knowledge, Truth, and Ideas*

Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz, “Meditationes de Cognitione, Veritate et Ideis” (1684), A VI iv 585–592; English translation in AG 23–27.

- Stable locations: A VI iv 585–592; AG 23–27
- Authority: Published primary text in the critical edition; scholarly English translation
- Supports: Distinctions among obscure, clear, confused, distinct, inadequate, adequate, symbolic, and intuitive cognition; analysis of concepts; possibility as absence of contradiction
- Limitation: Symbolic cognition is not identical to modern symbolic AI or computation. The text also marks limits on human adequate and intuitive cognition.

### S3 — *Discourse on Metaphysics*

Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz, *Discours de métaphysique* (1686), A VI iv 1529–1588; English translation in AG 35–68.

- Stable locations: §§5–9, 13–14, and 30; especially A VI iv 1537–1541, 1547–1550, 1575–1576
- Authority: Primary text in the critical edition; scholarly English translation
- Supports: Simplicity of hypotheses and richness of phenomena; complete individual concepts; each substance's expression of the universe; certainty distinguished from necessity; divine choice among possible orders
- Limitation: The complete-concept doctrine is metaphysical and theological. It is not a database schema, executable specification, or modern semantics of possible worlds.

### S4 — Texts on Expression

Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz, “Quid sit idea” (“What Is an Idea?”, probably autumn 1677), and letter to Antoine Arnauld, October 9, 1687.

- Stable locations: “What Is an Idea?” at A VI iv 1369–1371; letter to Arnauld at G II 112, with English translation in the scholarly Leibniz–Arnauld correspondence
- Authority: Primary philosophical text and correspondence; original-language wording requires critical-edition inspection before quotation
- Supports: Expression as an ordered or regulated correspondence in which relations in one thing answer to relations in another; expression need not be simple resemblance
- Limitation: Leibniz uses expression across mathematical, perceptual, and metaphysical settings. One definition does not make those domains identical or establish a universal modern representation theory.

### S5 — *General Inquiries about the Analysis of Concepts and Truths*

Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz, *Generales inquisitiones de analysi notionum et veritatum* (1686), A VI iv 739–788; English translation in LLP.

- Stable locations: A VI iv 739–788; corresponding sections in LLP
- Authority: Primary logical manuscript in the critical edition; scholarly translation by Parkinson
- Supports: Analysis of concepts and propositions, containment, identity, substitution, possibility, and experiments with a calculus of concepts
- Limitation: The text remained unpublished until the modern editorial recovery of Leibniz's logical papers. It records one major approach, not a finished universal system or demonstrated historical influence on nineteenth-century logic.

### S6 — Characteristic, Signs, and the Calculus of Reasoning

Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz, “Dialogue” (1677), texts collected under projects for universal science and a universal characteristic, *Ad constitutionem scientiae generalis* (probably spring 1682), and “De arte characteristica ad perficiendas scientias ratione nitentes” (probably summer 1688).

- Stable locations: “Dialogue,” A VI iv 20–25 and AG 268–272; *Ad constitutionem scientiae generalis*, first draft, A VI iv 449–451, especially 450; “De arte characteristica,” A VI iv 909–915, especially 913–914; other characteristic and universal-science texts at G VII 183–205
- Authority: Primary texts and project fragments; each candidate requires its own manuscript, date, edition, and translator record
- Supports: Human reasoning uses signs; characters can make relations among concepts inspectable; Leibniz proposed forms of analysis, judgment, discovery, and calculation
- Limitation: Titles and dates vary across editors, and the projects do not constitute one completed language or calculus. A VI iv 450 distinguishes errors of calculation from omissions in the matter supplied; the separate text at A VI iv 913–914 restricts the proposed art to what can be elicited or probabilistically determined from given information. *Characteristica universalis*, *calculus ratiocinator*, *scientia generalis*, and mathematical calculus may not be treated as synonyms.

### S7 — Contingency and Possible Worlds

Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz, “De contingentia” (“On Contingency,” probably spring to autumn 1689), A VI iv 1649–1652; correspondence with Arnauld; *Monadology* §§31–45; *Theodicy* §§414–416.

- Stable locations: A VI iv 1649–1652 and AG 28–30; G II 45 and AG 75; G VI 612–615 and AG 217–219; G VI 363–364
- Authority: Primary manuscripts, correspondence, and published work; chronology and translator must remain explicit
- Supports: Finite and infinite analysis; contingency; compossibility; unactualized possibilities; possible worlds in the divine understanding; God's selection of the actual world
- Limitation: Leibniz does not present possible worlds as independently running universes, computational state spaces, or a fully explicit modern possible-world semantics. Scholarly interpretations of essentialism and contingency remain disputed.

### S8 — Mathematical Calculus

Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz, “Nova methodus pro maximis et minimis, itemque tangentibus” (1684), *Acta Eruditorum*, October 1684, 467–473.

- Stable location: October 1684 *Acta Eruditorum*, 467–473; GM V 220–226; DOI catalog record 10.14711/spcol/b540295. Series VII currently publishes mathematical writings only through 1676, so no A VII locator for this 1684 article is presently available.
- Authority: Published primary mathematical text; the public-domain 1858 scan of GM V 220–226 was directly inspected through Internet Archive item `leibnizensmathe07leibgoog`
- Supports: Leibniz defines differentials relative to curves and tangents; gives rules for sums, products, quotients, powers, and roots; treats maxima, minima, and inflection through sign and differential conditions; and calls the method an algorithm of differential calculation
- Exact anchors: `calculi regulae` and the regression caution at GM V 220 / *Acta Eruditorum* 467; `algorithmum ... calculi ... differentialem` at GM V 222 / *Acta Eruditorum* 469
- Boundary: The article cautions that regression from a differential equation is not always available without qualification. Its objects and operations are geometrical and algebraic, unlike Q6's program for judgment from supplied conceptual premises.
- Limitation: The mathematical calculus neither supplies nor proves the universal characteristic, a calculus of concepts, possible-world metaphysics, or mechanical completion of interpretation.

### S11 — Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy: Leibniz's Philosophy of Mind

Mark Kulstad and Laurence Carlin, “Leibniz's Philosophy of Mind,” *The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*, substantive revision June 29, 2020.

- URL: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/leibniz-mind/
- Accessed: August 14, 2026
- Inspected sections: 1 Matter and Thought; 2 Denial of Mind-Body Interaction, Assertion of Pre-established Harmony; 3 Language and Mind; 4 Perception and Appetition
- Authority: Peer-reviewed specialist overview
- Supports: Expression as regulated relation; signs and reasoning; universal-characteristic projects; perception, unity, appetition, and pre-established harmony; modern computational readings
- Specific findings: (a) The letter to Arnauld of October 9, 1687, G II 112 / LA 144, is quoted as Leibniz's own definition of expression: one thing expresses another when there is a constant and regulated relation between what can be said of each; this is the scholarly formulation supporting Q2. (b) A third *calculemus* passage is quoted from *The Art of Discovery* (1685), C 176 / W 51, distinct from Q6a and Q6b and not currently mapped to an A locator; it is recorded here as a further-inspection candidate rather than admitted. - Limitation: The entry notes that the universal language was not actualized. Its own comparison to computational conceptions is contemporary interpretation, not a Leibnizian category.

### S14a — Inspected Specialist Interpretations

The current pass directly inspected the substantive sections, not just the bibliographies, of S9–S12.

- **Expression:** S11 defines expression through a constant and regulated relation and places it within Leibniz's metaphysics of simple substances, perception, and pre-established harmony. It therefore supports a relational reading while blocking a free-standing modern representation theory.
- **Language and logic:** S11 reconstructs the universal characteristic as representing both inferential form and conceptual content, but explicitly says Leibniz never actualized the project. S12 distinguishes affinity and later reception from initial historical influence: the major nineteenth-century calculi were already established before the decisive publication of Leibniz's logical manuscripts.
- **Modality:** S10 says Leibniz had resources that can be translated into possible-world semantics but did not explicitly formulate that semantics. It also records live disputes over superessentialism, transworld identity, and the relation between divine choice and striving possibles.
- **Strong-formalist objection:** S12 records Trendelenburg's objection that logical calculation presupposes a completed analysis of concepts; without it, the proposed characters rest on conjecture. This is a historical reception argument, not automatically Leibniz's own limit claim, but it directly contests a strong completion reading.
- **Boundary:** These inspected reference works establish defensible interpretive positions and disputes. They do not replace the queued monographs in S14 for any claim that depends on their distinctive arguments.

### S15 — Mathematics-History Boundary Sources

Eberhard Knobloch, “Leibniz and the Brachistochrone,” *Documenta Mathematica*, Extra Volume ISMP (2012): 15–18, DOI 10.4171/DMS/6/5; J. J. O'Connor and E. F. Robertson, “Gottfried Wilhelm von Leibniz,” *MacTutor History of Mathematics Archive*, last updated October 1998; Samuel V. Lemley, “Printing Leibniz's *Calculus*: Dating and Numbering the Editions of the *Nova Methodus pro Maximis et Minimis* (October 1684),” *The Library* 22, no. 2 (2021): 177–196, DOI 10.1093/library/22.2.177.

- URLs: https://ems.press/doi/10.4171/dms/6/5, https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/Biographies/Leibniz/, and https://doi.org/10.1093/library/22.2.177
- Authority: Knobloch is a specialist historian and editor of Leibniz's mathematics; his four-page chapter was directly inspected from the open EMS PDF. MacTutor is a university reference archive. Lemley's abstract and bibliographic record, but not full article, were inspected.
- Supports: The 1684 article's publication context, notation, operational rules, and lack of proofs; the later use of differential calculus on the brachistochrone; and the complex printing history of the original article. Knobloch records that Leibniz obtained the correct differential equation in 1696 but did not identify the curve as a cycloid until Johann Bernoulli informed him.
- Boundary: Successful formal derivation did not by itself supply every mathematical identification in this episode. The later differential-calculus application and priority dispute remain historically distinct from the characteristic and logical projects documented in S5–S6.
- Limitation: Knobloch's chapter is a focused case study, not a general history of all Leibnizian calculi; Lemley's full bibliographical argument remains uninspected.

## Prohibited Inferences

The current sources do not warrant claims that:

- Leibniz completed a universal characteristic or one universal calculus of reasoning
- *calculemus* means every disagreement is formally decidable
- mathematical notation, logical form, conceptual content, and metaphysical reality are interchangeable
- possible worlds are modern state spaces, databases, simulations, or concrete parallel universes
- complete concepts make human interpretation exhaustive or human knowledge divine
- the principle of sufficient reason is an executable search procedure
- expression always preserves meaning, reference, truth, or warranted action
- Leibniz's unpublished logical manuscripts caused systems developed before those manuscripts became available
- anticipation, influence, similarity, and identity name the same historical relation
- symbolic success eliminates purpose, judgment, theology, metaphysics, or interpretation
- Leibniz anticipated computers, artificial intelligence, transformers, embeddings, or geometric semantics
- Leibniz endorsed Book Three's closure contract or theory of derived meaning
