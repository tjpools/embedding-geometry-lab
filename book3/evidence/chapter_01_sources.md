## Source Standard

This ledger separates Berkeley's texts, scholarly interpretations, Book Three's reconstruction, and contemporary application. Section references to the *Principles*, *New Theory of Vision*, *De Motu*, and *The Analyst* are stable across the editions used here. References to the *Three Dialogues* require dialogue plus edition page or a quoted locator because that work is not divided into numbered sections.

Online transcriptions support research and location checking. Any quotation entering the manuscript must also be collated against A. A. Luce and T. E. Jessop's standard edition or Douglas M. Jesseph's scholarly edition of *De Motu* and *The Analyst*. Modernized reading texts may clarify syntax but are not quotation authorities.

## Primary Sources

### S1 — *A Treatise Concerning the Principles of Human Knowledge*

George Berkeley, *A Treatise concerning the Principles of Human Knowledge*, first published 1710; text based on the 1734 edition, edited by David R. Wilkins.

- URL: https://www.maths.tcd.ie/~dwilkins/Berkeley/HumanKnowledge/1734/HumKno.html
- Stable locations: Introduction 6–25; PHK 1–4, 18–33, 43–44, 58–66, 101–110, 118–133, 139–140
- Authority: Primary text transcribed from the 1734 edition and hosted by Trinity College Dublin
- Supports: Berkeley's critique of abstract general ideas; his distinction between ideas and spirits; ordinary objects as collections of ideas; laws of nature as regular methods; natural philosophy as discovery of analogies and general rules enabling conjecture and prediction
- Limitation: The anti-abstraction argument does not show that words must always evoke determinate ideas. Berkeley explicitly rejects that stronger theory in Introduction 19–20. His account of natural philosophy remains embedded in immaterialism and divine agency.

### S2 — *An Essay Towards a New Theory of Vision*

George Berkeley, *An Essay towards a New Theory of Vision*, first published 1709; text based on the final lifetime edition of 1732, edited by David R. Wilkins.

- URL: https://www.maths.tcd.ie/~dwilkins/Berkeley/Vision/1732B/Vision.html
- Stable locations: NTV 1–17, 41–45, 51, 59, 109, 147, 154–158
- Authority: Primary text transcribed from the 1732 edition and hosted by Trinity College Dublin
- Supports: Distance is not immediately seen; visible ideas suggest tangible ideas through learned, contingent connections; vision guides expectation and action; Berkeley calls the proper objects of vision a language of the Author of Nature
- Limitation: Berkeley's visual-language thesis is theological and sense-specific. It does not establish a general computational theory of meaning, and it does not identify visual signs with words, embeddings, or internal model representations.

### S3 — *De Motu*

George Berkeley, *De Motu: Sive, de Motus Principio & Natura, et de Causa Communicationis Motuum*, London, 1721. Standard English translation in *The Works of George Berkeley, Bishop of Cloyne*, vol. 4, translated by A. A. Luce and edited by A. A. Luce and T. E. Jessop, 1948–1957, 31–52. Scholarly modern edition: Douglas M. Jesseph, trans. and ed., *De Motu and The Analyst*, 1992.

- Reading copy: https://www.earlymoderntexts.com/assets/pdfs/berkeley1721.pdf
- Stable locations: DM 17–18, 23–24, 29, 33, 35–36
- Authority: Primary text; Luce's translation is part of the standard scholarly edition, and Jesseph supplies a dedicated modern edition with commentary
- Supports: Terms such as force, gravity, and attraction can aid reasoning and calculation without naming distinct physical qualities; utility in calculation differs from description of the nature of things; mechanical explanation reduces phenomena to experimentally grounded and generalized laws
- Limitation: The online reading copy is modernized and must not supply final quotations. Berkeley's restricted anti-realism about dynamical terms does not make him an instrumentalist about every scientific posit or every form of explanation.

### S4 — *The Analyst*

George Berkeley, *The Analyst; or, A Discourse Addressed to an Infidel Mathematician*, London and Dublin, 1734; first-edition text edited by David R. Wilkins.

- URL: https://www.maths.tcd.ie/pub/HistMath/People/Berkeley/Analyst/Analyst.html
- Stable locations: Analyst 1–10, 19–25, 31–37, 47–50, Queries 33–36 and 44–48
- Authority: Primary text based on the 1734 first editions and hosted by Trinity College Dublin
- Supports: Berkeley acknowledges the discovery and problem-solving power of fluxional methods while challenging their objects, principles, and inferences; he explicitly says his controversy concerns logic and method rather than the truth or usefulness of the conclusions; he distinguishes technically successful rule use from scientific demonstration
- Limitation: *The Analyst* is an argument in a religious controversy as well as a mathematical critique. It does not prove that calculus is invalid, that every successful method lacks warrant, or that later rigorous formulations inherit every defect Berkeley identified.

### S5 — *Three Dialogues Between Hylas and Philonous*

George Berkeley, *Three Dialogues between Hylas and Philonous*, first published 1713; text based primarily on the 1734 edition, edited by David R. Wilkins.

- URL: https://www.maths.tcd.ie/~dwilkins/Berkeley/Hylas/
- Stable locations: 1713 Preface; First Dialogue; Third Dialogue, especially Luce–Jessop pages 206, 212–215, 231–238, 247, and 253
- Authority: Primary text following the 1734 edition, with the original 1713 preface, hosted by Trinity College Dublin
- Supports: Berkeley presents immaterialism as opposed to skepticism and as preserving ordinary sensible objects; he distinguishes immediate perception from mistaken inference; he defends spirits and divine agency as positive parts of his ontology
- Limitation: Philonous is Berkeley's spokesperson within a staged dialogue, not a detachable source of aphorisms. Page references must be checked against the edition cited in the manuscript.

## Scholarly and Historical Sources

### S6 — Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy: George Berkeley

Lisa Downing and David Hilbert, "George Berkeley," *The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*, first published 2004, substantive revision August 12, 2026.

- URL: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/berkeley/
- Accessed: August 14, 2026
- Authority: Peer-reviewed scholarly reference work with an extensive primary and secondary bibliography
- Supports: Reconstruction of Berkeley's anti-materialist arguments, positive ontology, visual theory, natural philosophy, and *De Motu*; the interpretive case for a restricted instrumentalist attitude toward dynamics; objections to the master argument; the warning that Berkeley is not thereby a general instrumentalist
- Limitation: This is interpretation, not primary evidence. Its formulations must not be attributed to Berkeley, and live disputes must remain marked as disputes.

### S7 — Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy: Berkeley on Meaning

Lisa Downing and David Hilbert, "Berkeley on Meaning," supplement to "George Berkeley," *The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*, revised 2026.

- URL: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/berkeley/meaning.html
- Accessed: August 14, 2026
- Authority: Peer-reviewed scholarly reference work
- Supports: Berkeley's movement away from the unrestricted claim that every meaningful word must suggest an idea; general representation in Principles Introduction 16–20; practical and action-guiding significance in *Alciphron*; limits of a simple idea-naming account of meaning
- Limitation: The supplement reconstructs a development across Berkeley's works. The chapter may not compress that development into one timeless doctrine or treat practical significance as Book Three's later thesis of derived meaning.

### S8 — MacTutor History of Mathematics: George Berkeley

J. J. O'Connor and E. F. Robertson, "George Berkeley," *MacTutor History of Mathematics Archive*, University of St Andrews, last updated February 2005.

- URL: https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/Biographies/Berkeley/
- Accessed: August 14, 2026
- Authority: University-hosted history of mathematics reference
- Supports: Historical placement of *The Analyst* in the calculus controversy; Berkeley's focus on logical foundations despite true results; the controversy's role in prompting clarification and responses, including Maclaurin's
- Limitation: This overview does not adjudicate every mathematical or historiographical dispute. It cannot support the claim that Berkeley single-handedly caused the later rigorous foundation of calculus or that all details of his compensating-errors account were correct.

## Prohibited Inferences

The sources do not warrant claims that:

- Berkeley anticipated transformers, embeddings, attention, machine learning, or geometric semantics
- Berkeley endorsed the closure contract, evidence-bounded commitment, or Book Three's thesis of derived meaning
- operational or predictive success is epistemically worthless
- useful language is automatically true, referential, justified, or responsibly actionable
- Berkeley denied ordinary objects, reduced reality to private fantasy, or omitted spirits and God from his ontology
- Berkeley was an instrumentalist about all science, mathematics, language, or ontology
- *The Analyst* refuted calculus or showed that correct conclusions can never arise from defective reasoning
- Berkeley's compensating-errors account is the accepted modern explanation of why calculus works
- later rigorous calculus vindicates every eighteenth-century derivation Berkeley criticized
- the master argument, likeness principle, anti-abstractionism, or immaterialism is established merely because it creates pressure for an opponent
- visual ideas, mathematical signs, words, and transformer representations are the same kind of sign
- a historical analogy transfers Berkeley's authority to a contemporary technical or normative claim
