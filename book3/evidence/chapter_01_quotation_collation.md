## Collation Standard

Each record separates Berkeley's words from the claim Book Three may draw from them. Stable section or query numbers control location. Printed pages identify the edition inspected. Quotations use readable modern letterforms while preserving wording, capitalization, and punctuation except where a textual note records a change.

The comparison witnesses are:

- George Berkeley, *De Motu and The Analyst*, ed. and trans. Douglas M. Jesseph (Dordrecht: Kluwer Academic Publishers, 1992), critically established *Analyst* text, 159–220
- George Berkeley, *A Treatise concerning the Principles of Human Knowledge* and *Three Dialogues between Hylas and Philonous* (London: Jacob Tonson, 1734), public scan, Wellcome Library copy, Internet Archive `b30539158`
- George Berkeley, *An Essay towards a New Theory of Vision*, second edition (Dublin: Aaron Rhames, 1709), public scan, Internet Archive `bim_eighteenth-century_an-essay-towards-a-new-t_berkeley-george-bishop-_1709`
- David R. Wilkins's Trinity College Dublin transcriptions, used as independent location and transcription checks

The restricted Luce–Jessop scans were located but were not available for page inspection. Their page locators are therefore not represented as independently checked here.

## Collated Passages

### Q1 — Successful conclusions do not settle method

> I have no Controversy about your Conclusions, but only about your Logic and Method. [...] It must be remembered that I am not concerned about the truth of your Theorems, but only about the way of coming at them; whether it be legitimate or illegitimate, clear or obscure, scientific or tentative.

- **Work and location:** *The Analyst* §20
- **Edition and page:** Jesseph, 180–181
- **Comparison:** Wording checked against Jesseph's critically established text and the TCD first-edition transcription. The ellipsis omits Berkeley's intervening questions about objects, principles, and their application.
- **Textual divergence:** None material to the quoted clauses. Scan OCR corrupts several words and is not the quotation source.
- **Supported claim:** Berkeley expressly distinguishes the truth of mathematical conclusions from the legitimacy and clarity of the route by which they are reached.
- **Warrant:** Direct statement of the object of Berkeley's controversy.
- **Boundary:** It does not establish that the conclusions are false, that all fluxional derivations fail, or that operational success has no evidential value.
- **Status:** Verified for manuscript use

### Q2 — Truth can be reached without science

> Whether there be not a way of arriving at Truth, although the Principles are not scientific, nor the Reasoning just? And whether such a way ought to be called a Knack or a Science?

> Whether there can be Science of the Conclusion, where there is not Evidence of the Principles?

- **Work and location:** *The Analyst*, Queries 35–36
- **Edition and page:** Jesseph, 216
- **Comparison:** Wording checked against Jesseph's critically established text and the TCD transcription.
- **Textual divergence:** Jesseph records that the first London edition prints a defective reading for both occurrences of *Evidence* in Query 36. The 1734 Dublin edition and Berkeley's hand-corrected London copy support *Evidence*, which Jesseph adopts. Only the first occurrence is quoted here.
- **Supported claim:** Berkeley distinguishes arriving at a true result from possessing scientific warrant grounded in understood principles and just reasoning.
- **Warrant:** The paired queries explicitly contrast truth, method, evidence, and science.
- **Boundary:** The interrogative form and polemical setting must remain visible. The passage does not supply Book Three's closure contract or a general theory of warranted action.
- **Status:** Verified for manuscript use

### Q3 — Natural rules extend prediction

> [R]ules grounded on the Analogy, and Uniformness observed in the Production of natural Effects [...] extend our Prospect beyond what is present, and near to us, and enable us to make very probable Conjectures [...] as well as to predict Things to come.

- **Work and location:** *A Treatise concerning the Principles of Human Knowledge* §105
- **Edition and page:** 1734 Tonson edition, 125
- **Comparison:** Wording checked against the 1734 scan and the TCD transcription based on that edition. Brackets regularize the excerpt's initial letter; ellipses remove examples without joining different claims.
- **Textual divergence:** Long *s* is rendered as modern *s*. No material wording difference was found.
- **Supported claim:** Berkeley assigns natural philosophy a legitimate predictive role through observed analogy and uniformity.
- **Warrant:** The passage directly states what general rules enable investigators to do.
- **Boundary:** Berkeley embeds this role in divine governance and does not present prediction as demonstration, efficient-causal knowledge, or sufficient warrant for unrestricted ontology.
- **Status:** Verified for manuscript use

### Q4 — Analogy must not outrun evidence

> But we should proceed warily in such Things: for we are apt to lay too great a Stress on Analogies, and to the prejudice of Truth, humour that Eagerness of the Mind, whereby it is carried to extend its Knowledge into general Theorems.

> [I]n arguing from general Rules of Nature, it is not impossible we may extend the Analogy too far, and by that means run into Mistakes.

- **Work and location:** *Principles* §§106, 108
- **Edition and pages:** 1734 Tonson edition, 125–126 and 128
- **Comparison:** Wording checked against the 1734 scan and TCD transcription. The second excerpt's initial letter is regularized in brackets.
- **Textual divergence:** Long *s* is rendered as modern *s*. No material wording difference was found.
- **Supported claim:** Berkeley accepts generalization from phenomena while warning that analogy can be extended beyond what truth permits.
- **Warrant:** Both passages state the risk directly; §107 adds that deductions from natural rules are not demonstrations because uniform operation cannot be evidently known.
- **Boundary:** This is not a rejection of general rules, experiment, or prediction. The surrounding account retains God, final causes, and mind or spirit as positive explanatory commitments.
- **Status:** Verified for manuscript use

### Q5 — Error can enter through inference rather than perception

> He is not mistaken in regard to the Ideas he actually perceives; but in the Inferences he makes from his present Perceptions. [...] But his Mistake lies not in what he perceives immediately and at present [...] but in the wrong Judgment he makes concerning the Ideas he apprehends to be connected with those immediately perceived.

- **Work and location:** *Three Dialogues between Hylas and Philonous*, Third Dialogue
- **Edition and pages:** 1734 Tonson edition, 306–307; standard Luce–Jessop locator, volume 2, 237, not independently page-inspected in this pass
- **Comparison:** Wording checked against the 1734 scan and TCD transcription. Ellipses omit Berkeley's oar, tower, and moon examples and one parenthetical sentence.
- **Textual divergence:** Long *s* is rendered as modern *s*. No material wording difference was found in the quoted clauses.
- **Supported claim:** Within Philonous's argument, Berkeley distinguishes present sensible appearance from a mistaken expectation about connected ideas under other conditions.
- **Warrant:** The dialogue makes inference and judgment, rather than immediate perception, the site of the stated mistake.
- **Boundary:** Philonous is a character in a staged anti-skeptical defense of immaterialism. The passage does not establish a general secular inference theory, and it must not be detached from Berkeley's defense of sensible bodies, spirits, and God.
- **Status:** Withheld from direct manuscript quotation pending Luce–Jessop page inspection; available as research evidence from the inspected 1734 edition

### Q6 — Calculation and ontology are different tasks

> These things serve mechanics and computation: but it is one thing to serve computation and mathematical demonstrations, and another to exhibit the nature of things.

- **Work and location:** *De Motu* §18
- **Edition and page:** Jesseph, translation, 80
- **Comparison:** Wording taken from Jesseph's critically established Latin text and modern translation. The modernized Early Modern Texts version was used only as a locator and does not supply the quotation.
- **Textual divergence:** None within the inspected scholarly edition. Jesseph translates Berkeley's contrast between service to mechanics and computation and disclosure of the nature of things; the English wording is Jesseph's translation, not Berkeley's original language.
- **Supported claim:** Berkeley explicitly distinguishes mathematical and calculational service from exhibiting the nature of things.
- **Warrant:** The distinction is stated directly in a discussion of resolving forces by a parallelogram.
- **Boundary:** The passage concerns mathematical treatments of force in mechanics. It does not make Berkeley an unrestricted instrumentalist or show that every useful scientific term lacks reference.
- **Status:** Verified for manuscript use

### Q7 — Visual signs guide embodied action

> [T]he proper Objects of Vision constitute an universal Language of the Author of Nature, whereby we are instructed how to regulate our Actions, in order to attain those things, that are necessary to the Preservation and Well-being of our Bodies, as also to avoid whatever may be hurtful and destructive of them.

- **Work and location:** *An Essay towards a New Theory of Vision* §147
- **Edition and page:** 1709 second edition, 156; final lifetime text checked in the 1732 *Alciphron* second-edition transcription
- **Comparison:** The quoted wording is stable between the inspected 1709 scan and TCD's transcription of the 1732 final lifetime version. The bracket regularizes the excerpt's initial letter.
- **Textual divergence:** The 1709 edition reads *the Language of Nature* in the running head and uses historical letterforms; the body of §147 contains the quoted formulation. TCD's 1732 transcription modernizes neither the theological attribution nor the action-guiding claim.
- **Supported claim:** Berkeley presents vision as a learned sign system that guides bodily preservation and practical conduct.
- **Warrant:** Section 147 states both the sign relation and its action-guiding end; §§45, 59, and 85–87 supply the preceding account of habitual connection, foresight, and bodily use.
- **Boundary:** The thesis is theological, embodied, and specific to Berkeley's account of vision. It is not a general computational semantics and does not equate visual ideas with words, embeddings, or model representations.
- **Status:** Verified for manuscript use

## Attribution Check

The seven records support a historical sequence that remains Berkeley's: successful conclusions can coexist with disputed method; science demands evidence and understood principles; calculation and ontology are different tasks; observed regularities enable prediction; analogy can outrun its warrant; visual signs guide embodied action; and mistaken judgment can arise in inference from present perception.

The first sentence that belongs to Book Three rather than Berkeley is:

> Demonstrated operation constrains interpretation, but does not by itself settle ontology, meaning, or warranted action.

That sentence is a reconstruction. None of Q1–Q7 attributes closure, derived meaning, transformers, or a modern action theory to Berkeley.

| Check | Result | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Exact text and stable location | Pass for Q1–Q7 | Each record identifies a work and section, query, or dialogue location. |
| Required edition authority | Pass for Q1–Q4 and Q6–Q7; withheld for Q5 | Q5 may inform paraphrase but may not enter the manuscript as a direct quotation. |
| Claim narrower than evidence | Pass | Each supported claim stays within the quoted passage. |
| Historical and theological context | Pass with drafting obligation | *The Analyst*'s religious controversy and Berkeley's spirits, God, and divine governance must remain explicit. |
| Reconstruction boundary | Pass | The first Book Three sentence is quoted above and labeled as reconstruction. |
| Prohibited modern attribution | Pass | No quotation is used to attribute closure, derived meaning, or computational foresight to Berkeley. |
