## Collation Standard

Each candidate below is located by the original A or B pagination of the *Critique of Pure Reason* and, where relevant, by the corresponding Akademie volume and page. The A and B editions are treated as distinct witnesses, not as interchangeable pagination labels. The German text and A/B page numbers identify the primary passage; they do not establish an English translation or an uncontested interpretation.

No English wording below is presented as a quotation. German anchors identify the passage without reproducing the critical text at length. A candidate becomes manuscript-admissible only after the named translation is page-inspected, compared with the German text, and recorded with any material divergence between the A and B versions.

## Candidate Passages

### Q1 — Space and time are forms of sensibility

- **Work and date:** *Critique of Pure Reason* (1781; revised 1787), Transcendental Aesthetic
- **Original language:** German
- **Critical location:** A22–25 / B37–40 for space; A30–32 / B46–48 for time; A42–49 / B59–73 for the general observations
- **Original-text anchors:** *Der Raum ist kein empirischer Begriff*; *Die Zeit ist kein empirischer Begriff*; *der Raum ist nichts anderes, als nur die Form aller Erscheinungen äußerer Sinne*; *die Zeit ist nichts anderes, als die Form des inneren Sinnes*
- **Translation to inspect:** Guyer and Wood, *Critique of Pure Reason* (1998), 122–143
- **Context check:** Kant argues that space and time are neither learned general concepts nor properties that would remain unchanged when all subjective conditions of intuition are removed. They are forms of human sensibility, while empirical objects remain spatial and temporal for experience.
- **Supported claim:** Kant treats the formal conditions of sensibility as necessary for human experience without reducing them to empirical representations or external objects.
- **Boundary:** The Aesthetic does not by itself establish the categories, the unity of apperception, or the full limits of theoretical knowledge. It also does not identify a priori form with architecture, hardware, or pre-training.
- **Status:** Withheld pending German-page comparison and translation inspection

### Q2 — The categories require a transcendental deduction

- **Work and date:** *Critique of Pure Reason* (1781 A edition; 1787 B edition), Transcendental Deduction
- **Original language:** German
- **Critical location:** A84–130, especially A93–94; B116–169, especially B126
- **Original-text anchors:** *durch sie allein ist Erfahrung möglich*; *Bedingungen der Möglichkeit der Erfahrungen*; *die objektive Gültigkeit der Kategorien*
- **Translation to inspect:** Guyer and Wood, *Critique of Pure Reason* (1998), 200–248
- **Context check:** Kant asks not merely whether the categories can be listed, but what entitles them to apply objectively to objects of experience. The A and B Deductions pursue this problem through substantially different argumentative organizations.
- **Supported claim:** Kant presents the categories as a priori conditions of the possibility of experience and makes their objective validity the burden of a transcendental deduction.
- **Boundary:** The claim does not establish that the Deduction succeeds by one uncontested argument. A/B differences and competing reconstructions must remain visible.
- **Status:** Withheld pending separate A/B comparison and translation inspection

### Q3 — Apperception and the synthesis of representations

- **Work and date:** *Critique of Pure Reason* (1787 B edition)
- **Original language:** German
- **Critical location:** B130–133; B142; B162
- **Original-text anchors:** *Das: Ich denke, muß alle meine Vorstellungen begleiten können*; *wir können uns nichts als verbunden in dem Objekt vorstellen, ohne es vorher selbst verbunden zu haben*; *Ein Urteil ist nichts anders, als die Art, gegebenen Erkenntnissen die objektive Einheit der Apperzeption zu geben*
- **Translation to inspect:** Guyer and Wood, *Critique of Pure Reason* (1998), 232–236 and 240–241
- **Context check:** Kant links the unity of apperception to the act of combining a manifold of representations. The B Deduction then distinguishes necessary objective connection from merely subjective association and treats judgment as a way of bringing representations to the objective unity of apperception.
- **Supported claim:** For Kant, objective judgment involves a synthesis governed by the unity of apperception rather than a mere accumulation of associated contents.
- **Boundary:** Apperception is a transcendental condition in Kant's argument, not a description of a cognitive architecture, control loop, attention mechanism, or integrated software implementation.
- **Status:** Withheld pending German-page comparison and translation inspection

### Q4 — Schemata mediate categories and intuition

- **Work and date:** *Critique of Pure Reason* (1781; revised 1787), Schematism and Analytic of Principles
- **Original language:** German
- **Critical location:** A137–147 / B176–187; A148–235 / B187–294; B274–279 for the Refutation of Idealism
- **Original-text anchors:** *die sinnliche Bedingung, unter welcher allein reine Verstandesbegriffe gebraucht werden können*; *das Schema eines reinen Verstandesbegriffs*; *Grundsätze des reinen Verstandes*
- **Translation to inspect:** Guyer and Wood, *Critique of Pure Reason* (1998), 271–381
- **Context check:** Kant's Schematism addresses how pure concepts can have sensible application. The following Principles organize the conditions under which appearances can be judged according to quantity, quality, relation, and modality. The Refutation of Idealism belongs specifically to the B edition.
- **Supported claim:** Kant does not treat categories as self-applying abstractions; their objective use requires a mediating relation to intuition and principles of possible experience.
- **Boundary:** Schematism is not a theory of representation formats, feature alignment, or machine interfaces. The B-edition Refutation of Idealism has its own argumentative status and must not be silently folded into the A text.
- **Status:** Withheld pending German-page comparison and translation inspection

### Q5 — Noumena mark a limit, not a hidden empirical object

- **Work and date:** *Critique of Pure Reason* (1781; revised 1787), Analytic of Concepts
- **Original language:** German
- **Critical location:** A235–260 / B294–315
- **Original-text anchors:** *Erscheinungen*; *Noumena*; *der Begriff eines Noumenon*; *die Kategorien haben gar keinen andern Gebrauch zum Erkenntnisse der Dinge, als ihre Anwendung auf Gegenstände der Erfahrung*
- **Translation to inspect:** Guyer and Wood, *Critique of Pure Reason* (1998), 382–408
- **Context check:** Kant distinguishes objects as appearances, which can be cognized under the conditions of human sensibility and understanding, from the limiting thought of things considered apart from those conditions. The passage is not a report of hidden empirical entities available to a better instrument.
- **Supported claim:** Kant restricts theoretical use of the categories to objects of possible experience and uses the noumenon concept to mark a limit on cognition.
- **Boundary:** This does not settle whether Kant's noumena are best understood as a second class of objects, a second aspect, or a limiting concept. It also does not license translating noumena into latent states or ground truth.
- **Status:** Withheld pending German-page comparison and specialist comparison

### Q6 — The Antinomies expose reason's overreach

- **Work and date:** *Critique of Pure Reason* (1781; revised 1787), Transcendental Dialectic
- **Original language:** German
- **Critical location:** A405–567 / B432–595
- **Original-text anchors:** *Antinomie der reinen Vernunft*; *die Welt hat einen Anfang in der Zeit*; *die Welt hat keinen Anfang*; *Auflösung der kosmologischen Ideen*
- **Translation to inspect:** Guyer and Wood, *Critique of Pure Reason* (1998), 462–548
- **Context check:** Kant presents paired arguments for opposed claims about the world as a totality, then diagnoses the conflict as arising when reason treats the world of appearances as a complete object in itself. The resolutions distinguish legitimate empirical inquiry from the illicit demand for an unconditioned totality available as theoretical knowledge.
- **Supported claim:** Kant uses the Antinomies to show how reason can generate apparently compelling contradictions by exceeding the conditions under which its concepts have objective use.
- **Boundary:** The Antinomies are not training failures, optimization pathologies, or evidence that reason is generally unreliable. Kant's distinction among constitutive and regulative uses must remain explicit.
- **Status:** Withheld pending German-page comparison and translation inspection

### Q7 — The critical question is the possibility of synthetic a priori judgment

- **Work and date:** *Prolegomena to Any Future Metaphysics* (1783)
- **Original language:** German
- **Critical location:** AA IV 255–257; 260–265; 275–294; 294–365
- **Original-text anchors:** *Wie ist reine Mathematik möglich?*; *Wie ist reine Naturwissenschaft möglich?*; *Wie ist Metaphysik überhaupt möglich?*
- **Translation to inspect:** Allison and Heath, *Theoretical Philosophy after 1781* (2002), *Prolegomena* section
- **Context check:** Kant recasts the critical project as a series of questions about how synthetic a priori cognition is possible. The *Prolegomena* is an accessible restatement and analytic presentation of the problem, not a replacement for the A/B Deduction.
- **Supported claim:** Kant frames transcendental philosophy as an inquiry into the conditions that make synthetic a priori judgments possible and into the boundaries of metaphysical cognition.
- **Boundary:** The formulation does not authorize a modern theory of cognition or a completed account of meaning. The *Prolegomena* is Kant's restatement and must not erase the historical and argumentative differences of the *Critique*.
- **Status:** Withheld pending German-page comparison and translation inspection

## Attribution Check

Q1–Q7 establish a bounded historical sequence: sensibility supplies forms; understanding supplies categories; synthesis and apperception bind representations into objective judgment; schemata mediate pure concepts and intuition; noumena mark a limit on theoretical cognition; the Antinomies display reason's overreach; and the *Prolegomena* restates the question of synthetic a priori knowledge.

The first sentence that belongs to Book Three rather than Kant is:

> Structured experience has conditions that neither perception nor formal relation supplies on its own, and any account of derived meaning must respect those conditions without impersonating them.

That sentence remains a reconstruction. None of Q1–Q7 attributes transformers, cognitive architectures, derived meaning, closure, or a general theory of warranted action to Kant.

The A/B distinction is itself part of the attribution boundary. Q2 and Q3 cannot be treated as one undifferentiated account of "the Deduction," and Q4's Refutation of Idealism is B-edition material. The two-objects and two-aspects interpretations are scholarly reconstructions, not interchangeable glosses on Kant's text.

## Bounded Transformer Comparison

The following comparison belongs to Book Three's contemporary application, not to the historical account of Kant:

| Kantian candidate | Historical claim | Transformer-side observation | Licensed comparison | Explicit non-identity |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Q2 — Transcendental Deduction | Kant argues for the objective validity of categories as conditions of possible experience; the A and B arguments remain distinct. | Attention layers transform an ordered token sequence using learned, context-sensitive relations. | A transformer can be studied as a learned structuring operation whose output depends on relational constraints across a sequence. | Attention is not a transcendental deduction, and its transformations do not establish objective validity, experience, or understanding. |
| Q3 — Apperception and synthesis | In the B Deduction, Kant links the unity of representations to synthesis and the possibility that the "I think" accompany representations. | Attention and residual pathways allow information from multiple token positions to influence later representations. | These pathways provide a bounded computational analogy for binding or combining representations. | They do not constitute consciousness, a self, a transcendental unity of apperception, or one representational act in Kant's sense. |
| Q4 — Schematism and principles | Kant treats the application of pure concepts to intuition as requiring mediating conditions and principles of possible experience. | Learned parameters and activation patterns constrain which transformations and continuations are available for a given input. | A model may be described as imposing learned structure on token sequences and producing coherent form under those constraints. | Learned operators are not Kantian categories or schemata; they do not guarantee truth, reference, intention, or warranted interpretation. |

The comparison therefore supports a narrower Book Three claim: a system can generate coherent, structured output without thereby supplying the conditions that would warrant calling the output understood, true, or fit for action. It does not support the stronger claims that transformers model intelligence, that attention is an artificial "I think," that learned geometry is a Kantian category system, or that hallucination is transcendental illusion. The relation is analogical and diagnostic: Kant helps name why structure and coherence do not by themselves settle reality or warrant.
