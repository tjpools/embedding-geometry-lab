## Candidate Passages

### Q1 — Symbolic cognition extends thought but can conceal contradiction

- **Work and date:** “Meditationes de Cognitione, Veritate, et Ideis” (1684)
- **Original language:** Latin
- **Critical location:** A VI iv 585–592, especially 587–588
- **Original-text anchors:** *rerum loco signis utimur*; *cogitationem caecam vel etiam symbolicam*; *lateat nos contradictio*
- **Translation to inspect:** Roger Ariew and Daniel Garber, trans., *Philosophical Essays* (1989), 23–27
- **Context check:** Leibniz says that in long analysis people commonly use signs in place of things and omit present explanation of those signs. He calls this blind or symbolic thought. The next paragraph warns that reliance on such thought without adequate resolution can leave a contradiction hidden.
- **Supported claim:** Leibniz treats symbolic cognition as an ordinary and powerful economy of thought while expressly denying that sign use by itself guarantees possession of an adequate or even possible concept.
- **Boundary:** The passage does not identify symbolic cognition with modern symbolic AI. It also blocks a simple inference from formally manageable signs to fully interpreted or contradiction-free concepts.
- **Status:** Withheld pending translation inspection

### Q2 — Expression preserves relations without requiring resemblance

- **Work and date:** “Quid sit idea” (“What Is an Idea?”, probably autumn 1677)
- **Original language:** Latin
- **Critical location:** A VI iv 1369–1371, especially 1370
- **Original-text anchors:** *habitudines, quae habitudinibus rei exprimendae respondent*; *non esse necessarium ut id quod exprimit simile sit rei expressae*
- **Translation to inspect:** Leroy E. Loemker, trans., *Philosophical Papers and Letters*, second edition (1969), 207–208
- **Context check:** Leibniz gives heterogeneous examples: a machine model, planar projection, speech, numerical characters, and an algebraic equation. What they share is a correspondence from relations in the expression to properties of the expressed thing; simple likeness is unnecessary if an analogy of relations is preserved.
- **Supported claim:** Expression is a structured correspondence that can support knowledge across unlike media.
- **Boundary:** The examples do not establish that every relation-preserving mapping preserves reference, truth, practical importance, or meaning in Book Three's sense. The essay finally grounds ideas in God as author of both things and minds.
- **Status:** Withheld pending translation inspection

### Q3 — Arbitrary characters can participate in non-arbitrary truth

- **Work and date:** “Dialogus” (“Dialogue,” August 1677)
- **Original language:** Latin
- **Critical location:** A VI iv 20–25, especially 23–25
- **Original-text anchors:** *si characteres abessent nunquam quicquam distincte cogitaremus*; *eorum tamen usus et connexio habet quiddam quod non est arbitrarium*
- **Translation to inspect:** Roger Ariew and Daniel Garber, trans., *Philosophical Essays* (1989), 268–272
- **Context check:** The dialogue asks how truth can be independent of human choice when definitions and signs are conventional. Its answer distinguishes arbitrary character choice from the order, connection, and proportional correspondence maintained in correct use.
- **Supported claim:** Leibniz distinguishes the conventional selection of signs from constraints on their relational use.
- **Boundary:** The dialogue does not say form manufactures truth or that any internally consistent notation correctly expresses its intended subject. Its examples presuppose a known relation between characters and things.
- **Status:** Withheld pending translation inspection

### Q4 — Complete concepts belong to a metaphysics of substance

- **Work and date:** *Discours de métaphysique* (*Discourse on Metaphysics*, early 1686), §8
- **Original language:** French
- **Critical location:** A VI iv 1540–1541
- **Original-text anchor:** *la nature d'une substance individuelle ... est d'avoir une notion si accomplie ... à comprendre et à en faire deduire tous les predicats du sujet*
- **Translation to inspect:** Roger Ariew and Daniel Garber, trans., *Philosophical Essays* (1989), 41
- **Context check:** The claim follows Leibniz's account of true predication and introduces individual substance. God sees in Alexander's individual notion the ground and reason of all predicates truly attributable to him.
- **Supported claim:** Leibniz connects truth about an individual with a complete concept that contains or grounds its predicates.
- **Boundary:** The subject is an individual substance known completely by God, not a finite data record available to a human reasoner. The doctrine raises, rather than mechanically resolves, questions of contingency and freedom.
- **Status:** Withheld pending translation inspection

### Q5 — Contingent truth does not terminate in finite demonstration

- **Work and date:** “De contingentia” (“On Contingency,” probably 1689)
- **Original language:** Latin
- **Critical location:** A VI iv 1649–1652, especially 1650
- **Original-text anchors:** *in contingentibus vero progressus est analyseos in infinitum, per rationes rationum*; *nunquam quidem habeatur plena demonstratio*
- **Translation to inspect:** Roger Ariew and Daniel Garber, trans., *Philosophical Essays* (1989), 28–30
- **Context check:** Leibniz contrasts necessary truths, whose analysis reaches an identical equation, with contingent truths, whose analysis continues through reasons for reasons without a complete demonstration. He says only God understands the infinite series perfectly in one act of mind.
- **Supported claim:** Analysis constrains contingent truth without making it finitely demonstrable to human reasoners.
- **Boundary:** Infinite analysis is embedded in Leibniz's account of God, creation, and contingency. It is not an indefinitely long computer run or permission to treat unexplained output as warranted.
- **Status:** Withheld pending translation inspection

### Q6 — *Calculemus* is bounded by the givens

- **Works and dates:** *Ad constitutionem scientiae generalis* (probably spring 1682), first draft; “De arte characteristica ad perficiendas scientias ratione nitentes” (probably summer 1688)
- **Original language:** Latin
- **Critical locations:** Q6a at A VI iv 450; Q6b at A VI iv 913–914
- **Original-text anchors:** Q6a: *sibi dicere possit: calculemus*; *error ... calculi, vel omissionis alicujus ex datis*. Q6b: *quaecunque ex datis ... possint elici*; *determinemus ipsum gradum probabilitatis*.
- **Translation to inspect:** No English translation has been selected or inspected for either text
- **Context check:** In the 1682 text Leibniz imagines disputants saying “let us calculate,” then distinguishes correctable errors of calculation from omissions in the supplied matter, assigning the latter to discovery rather than judgment. In the separate 1688 text he limits the art to what can be elicited from what is given and, where the answer is not determined, proposes approximation or determination of the degree of probability available from those givens.
- **Supported claim:** The slogan expresses a program for disciplined reasoning under supplied premises, not an unconditional promise that every dispute is mechanically decidable.
- **Boundary:** Both passages are programmatic, not reports of a completed universal method. Their shared theme does not make them one text, and they leave premise supply, omission, discovery, and underdetermination outside calculation's automatic control.
- **Status:** Withheld pending translation authority and inspection

### Q7 — Differential calculus is a specific mathematical operation

- **Work and date:** “Nova methodus pro maximis et minimis, itemque tangentibus” (published October 1684)
- **Original language:** Latin
- **Inspected location:** *Acta Eruditorum* 1684, 467–473, reprinted GM V 220–226; public-domain Gerhardt scan inspected through Internet Archive item `leibnizensmathe07leibgoog`
- **Original-text anchors:** GM V 220 / *Acta* 467: *calculi regulae* and *non dari semper regressum a differentiali Aequatione, nisi cum quadam cautione*. GM V 222 / *Acta* 469: *algorithmum ... calculi hujus, quem voco differentialem*.
- **Translation to inspect:** No edition-based English translation has been selected or inspected
- **Context check:** Leibniz defines differentials relative to ordinates, axes, and tangents; states operational rules for sums, products, quotients, powers, and roots; and applies differential and sign conditions to maxima, minima, and inflection. He also cautions that a return from a differential equation is not always available without qualification.
- **Supported claim:** The published differential calculus is an inspectable mathematical method with specified objects, notation, transformations, and geometrical applications.
- **Boundary:** This article does not identify its differential algorithm with the universal characteristic, conceptual analysis, or the program for resolving disputes in Q6. Shared words such as “calculus” and “algorithm” do not make the projects one system.
- **Status:** Admissible for paraphrase; direct English quotation withheld pending translation inspection

## Attribution Check

Q1–Q6 support a bounded historical sequence: signs extend finite cognition; relational correspondence need not be resemblance; conventional notation can preserve non-conventional constraints; complete concepts and analysis connect formal truth to metaphysics; contingent truths resist finite completion; and calculational judgment remains dependent on what has been supplied. Q7 separately establishes a published mathematical technique and prevents the differential calculus from being inferred out of that philosophical sequence.

The first sentence that belongs to Book Three rather than Leibniz is:

> Formal relations can constrain and extend interpretation without making interpretation mechanically complete.

That sentence remains a reconstruction. None of Q1–Q6 attributes transformers, derived meaning, closure, or a general theory of warranted action to Leibniz.
